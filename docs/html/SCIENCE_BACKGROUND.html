LunarSerpent — Lunar Surface Science and Exploration Insights

Document ID: LS-DOC-007 · Version: 1.0 · Status: Baseline Date: 2026-08-04 Covers: Project brief Part 6 prerequisite — "conduct full study of the Moon, the Moon's surface, and exploration insights, but only include insights that are scientifically provable."

0. Inclusion criterion

This document contains only statements that are (a) measured by an instrument, (b) derived from measurement by a documented method, or (c) directly observed during a flown mission.

Statements that are plausible but unproven are excluded, and a short list of commonly repeated claims that did not meet the criterion appears in §13. Each section closes with ⇒ Implication for traverse planning, which is the actionable output that feeds Part 6.

1. Global physical parameters

Property Value Source
Mean radius 1737.4 km Archinal et al. (2018)
Surface gravity 1.625 m s⁻² (0.1657 g⊕) NASA Moon Fact Sheet
Surface area 3.793 × 10⁷ km² Derived, 4πR²
Escape velocity 2.38 km s⁻¹ Derived
Sidereal rotation 27.32 d (synchronous) IAU
Solar day (synodic) 29.53 d IAU
Axial tilt to ecliptic 1.54° Archinal et al. (2018)
Surface pressure ~3 × 10⁻¹⁵ bar (hard vacuum) Apollo ALSEP
Topographic range −9.1 km to +10.8 km (19.9 km) LOLA LDEM_16 label

⇒ Implication for traverse planning. Three of these drive the mobility model directly:

  1. Gravity 1.625 m s⁻² enters the slope-resistance term of FORMULAS.md §7.1. A rover needs ~6× less power to climb a given grade than on Earth — but also has ~6× less normal force, so traction is reduced in proportion. The two effects cancel in θ_max = arctan(μ), which is why gradeability is set by friction, not by gravity.
  2. Axial tilt of only 1.54° is the single most consequential number for polar operations. Because the spin axis is nearly perpendicular to the ecliptic, the Sun never rises more than ~1.5° above the horizon at the poles. This creates both permanently shadowed regions and near-permanently illuminated peaks (§9).
  3. The 29.53-day solar day means ~14.75 Earth-days of darkness at most latitudes — a hard constraint on solar-powered traverse duration.

2. The nearside–farside dichotomy

Measured facts:

Property Nearside Farside
Mare coverage ~31 % ~2 %
Mean elevation lower higher by ~1.9 km
Crustal thickness (GRAIL) ~30 km ~50 km

Verified independently during this project: a range-request read of LOLA LDEM_64 at (0 °N, 180 °E) — the farside centre — returned +2730 m, consistent with the elevated farside highlands.

⇒ Implication. Terrain character is predictably different by hemisphere. Nearside maria offer the gentlest traverses; farside highlands are rougher and more heavily cratered, because they are older and lack the resurfacing that mare flooding provided.

Wieczorek, M. A. et al. (2013), The crust of the Moon as seen by GRAIL, Science 339, 671–675. DOI: 10.1126/science.1231530

3. Surface units: maria and highlands

Property Maria Highlands
Composition Basalt (Fe, Ti-rich) Anorthosite (Ca, Al-rich)
Albedo 0.07 – 0.10 0.11 – 0.18
Age 3.1 – 3.8 Ga 4.4 – 4.5 Ga
Crater density Lower Higher (saturated)
Regolith depth 4 – 5 m 10 – 15 m
Typical km-scale slope < 3° 5 – 15°

⇒ Implication. Mare surfaces are the preferred traverse medium: younger, hence fewer craters; flatter; thinner and better-characterised regolith. Every crewed Apollo landing that used the LRV operated on or adjacent to mare terrain. Highlands cost more energy and carry higher obstacle density.

McKay, D. S. et al. (1991), The lunar regolith, in Lunar Sourcebook, Cambridge University Press, Ch. 7. Bart, G. D. et al. (2011), Global survey of lunar regolith depths from LROC images, Icarus 215, 485–490. DOI: 10.1016/j.icarus.2011.07.017

4. Regolith mechanical properties

The single most important body of knowledge for rover mobility, because it determines whether wheels grip or sink. These values come from Apollo core samples, Surveyor penetrometry and Lunokhod's onboard penetrometer — that is, direct in-situ measurement.

Property Value Note
Bulk density (0–15 cm) 1.50 ± 0.05 g cm⁻³ Increases with depth
Bulk density (30–60 cm) 1.74 ± 0.05 g cm⁻³ Self-compaction
Porosity 40 – 50 % Very loose at surface
Cohesion 0.1 – 1.0 kPa Low but non-zero
Friction angle 30° – 50° (typ. 35–45°) Governs gradeability
Median grain size 40 – 130 µm Silt-to-fine-sand
Fraction < 20 µm 10 – 20 % The problematic dust fraction
Bearing capacity (~10 cm depth) > 30 kPa Ample for rover loads

⇒ Implication — this is where θ_max comes from. With a friction angle of 35–45°, the theoretical no-slip limit is arctan(μ) with μ ≈ 0.3–0.6, giving 17°–31° (FORMULAS.md §6.1). Flight vehicles are limited more conservatively — VIPER ~15° sustained — because wheel slip rises sharply well before the theoretical limit, and because slip is measured, not assumed:

Apollo 15–17 direct observation: LRV wheel slip was negligible on level ground but rose to 2–3 % on modest slopes, and became operationally significant above ~15°. Crews reported difficulty maintaining traction on crater walls near 20°.

Bearing capacity is not a constraint — a rover will not sink. Slope and obstacles are the binding limits, which is precisely why LunarSerpent's hazard model is built on slope, roughness and boulders rather than on soil-strength trafficability.

Carrier, W. D., Olhoeft, G. R. & Mendell, W. (1991), Physical properties of the lunar surface, in Lunar Sourcebook, Cambridge University Press, Ch. 9. Mitchell, J. K. et al. (1972), Soil mechanics, Apollo 16 Preliminary Science Report, NASA SP-315.

5. Impact cratering — the process that made the surface

The Moon has no atmosphere, no plate tectonics and no liquid water. Impact cratering is the dominant landform-producing process, with mass wasting secondary. This is why a cratering-mechanics framework (Part 3) is scientifically correct here, rather than terrestrial geomorphology.

Established sequence: contact and compression → excavation (transient cavity) → modification (collapse, where gravity exceeds target strength).

Measured consequences used by this project:

Fact Value Used in
Simple→complex transition 15 – 20 km on the Moon FORMULAS.md §4.1, §4.3
Simple crater d/D ≈ 0.196 §4.1
Complex crater d/D Falls as D^−0.7 §4.1
Excavation depth ≈ 0.1 D §4.2
Continuous ejecta extent ≈ 1 crater radius beyond rim §6.3
Transition scales as ≈ g⁻¹ across bodies §4.1

⇒ Implication. Crater size predicts hazard character. A 100 m crater is a local obstacle. A 100 km crater is a terrain feature with a multi-kilometre ejecta apron, terraced walls at or beyond rover slope limits, and a raised rim. The exponential standoff of FORMULAS.md §6.3, scaled to crater radius, is the mathematical expression of exactly this.

Melosh, H. J. (1989), Impact Cratering: A Geologic Process, Oxford University Press. Pike, R. J. (1977), in Impact and Explosion Cratering, Pergamon, 489–509.

6. Boulders and ejecta blocks

Measured facts:

  • Boulders are concentrated around fresh craters, in ejecta deposits, and on steep slopes where mass wasting has exposed blocks.
  • Block abundance decays steeply with distance from the source crater.
  • Size–frequency follows a power law N(>D) ∝ D^−β, with β ≈ 2.5–3.5.
  • Boulders degrade over ~10⁷–10⁸ years by micrometeorite bombardment, so abundant boulders indicate a young surface.
  • Boulder tracks on slopes are directly observed in LROC NAC imagery, recording downslope rolling.

⇒ Implication — the highest-value planning insight in this document. Boulder hazard is predictable from crater age and proximity, not random. A traverse can be routed to avoid fresh-crater ejecta fields before any high-resolution imagery is examined, using only the crater catalogue and the degradation index. High-resolution boulder detection then confirms the prediction over a far smaller area, instead of searching blindly.

This is precisely the two-tier architecture adopted in Part 2: global catalogue-driven prediction, then AOI-scale confirmation.

Watkins, R. N. et al. (2019), Boulder distributions around young lunar impact craters, JGR Planets 124, 2754–2771. DOI: 10.1029/2019JE005963 Basilevsky, A. T. et al. (2013), Survival times of meter-sized boulders on the surface of the Moon, Planet. Space Sci. 89, 118–126. DOI: 10.1016/j.pss.2013.07.011

7. Topographic and slope statistics

Measured from LOLA:

Statistic Value Baseline
Global topographic range 19.9 km —
Median slope, maria < 2° ~1 km
Median slope, highlands 4° – 8° ~1 km
Slopes > 20° Rare — mostly crater walls and rilles ~1 km
RMS slope increases with Decreasing baseline self-affine

⇒ Implication — and a warning. Most of the Moon is gentle at kilometre baselines. The danger is concluding from this that the Moon is easy to drive on. It is not: hazards concentrate at metre scale, which kilometre-baseline statistics cannot see.

This is the empirical justification for the scale-dependence discipline enforced throughout LunarSerpent (FORMULAS.md §3.5): a slope value is meaningless without its baseline, and a 1.9 km-baseline slope map must never be presented as a rover-scale safety product.

Kreslavsky, M. A. et al. (2013), Lunar topographic roughness maps from LOLA data, Icarus 226, 52–66. DOI: 10.1016/j.icarus.2013.04.027 Rosenburg, M. A. et al. (2011), Global surface slopes and roughness of the Moon from LOLA, JGR Planets 116, E02001. DOI: 10.1029/2010JE003716

8. Thermal environment

Measured by LRO Diviner:

Location Daytime max Night min
Equator ~390 K (+117 °C) ~95 K (−178 °C)
Mid-latitudes ~360 K ~100 K
Permanently shadowed regions — 25 – 40 K

The coldest PSR floors are among the coldest measured surfaces in the Solar System — colder than Pluto's surface.

Regolith is an excellent insulator: below ~30 cm, temperature is nearly constant at ~250 K regardless of surface swings.

⇒ Implication. Thermal management, not terrain, often limits mission duration. A solar rover must either survive a ~354-hour night or complete its traverse within one lunar day. This makes traverse time a hard mission constraint, which is why FORMULAS.md §7.2 reports time and energy as first-class path metrics rather than incidental statistics.

Paige, D. A. et al. (2010), Diviner Lunar Radiometer observations of cold traps in the Moon's south polar region, Science 330, 479–482. DOI: 10.1126/science.1187726

9. Polar illumination — why the poles are the target

The 1.54° axial tilt (§1) produces conditions found nowhere else on the Moon:

Fact Value Source
Sun's maximum elevation at the poles ~1.5° Geometric consequence of tilt
PSR total area ~13 000 km² (S), ~17 000 km² (N) LOLA / Diviner
Best illuminated ridges 80 – 90 % annual illumination LOLA illumination modelling
Confirmed water ice in PSRs Yes — direct spectroscopic detection M³, LCROSS

⇒ Implications — several, and all operationally decisive:

  1. Illumination is a routing resource, not merely a constraint. Near-permanently lit ridges allow near-continuous solar power. A route that stays on them can operate indefinitely; one that strays into shadow starts a battery countdown.
  2. Shadows are long and they move. With the Sun ≤1.5° above the horizon, 10 m of relief casts a shadow hundreds of metres long, and that shadow sweeps as the Moon rotates. Illumination at a polar site is a function of time, not a static map.
  3. Excellent for boulder detection. The permanently grazing illumination that complicates operations makes shadow-length photoclinometry maximally sensitive (FORMULAS.md §5.2) — h_min = k·p·tan(e) is minimised at low solar elevation e. The poles are the easiest place on the Moon to detect small boulders.
  4. Cylindrical map projections fail here. As established in FORMULAS.md §2.4, east–west cell size → 0 at the pole. Polar work requires polar stereographic products — which is why LunarSerpent refuses cylindrical analysis above 85° rather than returning numbers it cannot justify.

Mazarico, E. et al. (2011), Illumination conditions of the lunar polar regions using LOLA topography, Icarus 211, 1066–1081. DOI: 10.1016/j.icarus.2010.10.030 Colaprete, A. et al. (2010), Detection of water in the LCROSS ejecta plume, Science 330, 463–468. DOI: 10.1126/science.1186986

10. Lunar dust — the dominant operational hazard

Directly observed during Apollo, and the most consistently reported operational problem across all crewed surface missions:

  • Particles are angular and abrasive — formed by micrometeorite fragmentation, with no aqueous or aeolian rounding to smooth them.
  • Electrostatically charged by UV photoemission and solar wind; adheres tenaciously.
  • Apollo 17 documented abrasion of suits and seals, obscured optics, degraded radiator performance causing thermal-control problems, and jammed mechanisms.
  • The LRV required an improvised fender repair on Apollo 17 after dust rooster-tails coated the vehicle.

⇒ Implication. Dust is a hazard LunarSerpent can only partially mitigate through routing: disturbed, fresh, fine-grained surfaces and steep slopes mobilise more dust. But honesty requires stating the limit — dust accumulation is a time-and-activity-dependent degradation process, not a spatial hazard field. It cannot be represented as a cost-map layer without inventing data. It is therefore documented as a mission-planning consideration and deliberately excluded from the cost surface.

Gaier, J. R. (2005), The effects of lunar dust on EVA systems during the Apollo missions, NASA/TM-2005-213610.

11. Radiation

Source Dose characteristics
Galactic cosmic rays Continuous, ~0.3 mSv/day at the surface
Solar particle events Sporadic; potentially lethal without shelter
Neutron albedo Secondary, from regolith interaction

The Moon has no magnetosphere and no atmosphere, so the surface is exposed. Regolith is an effective shield — roughly 1 m provides substantial protection.

⇒ Implication. Relevant to crewed mission planning (shelter proximity, sortie duration) but not a spatial routing constraint for a robotic rover at the scales this system models. Included for completeness; explicitly excluded from the cost surface.

Zeitlin, C. et al. (2013), Science 340, 1080–1084. DOI: 10.1126/science.1235989

12. Flown-mission experience — the empirical record

Everything below is observed operational history, not modelling.

Mission Vehicle Distance Speed Key lesson
Luna 17 / Lunokhod 1 (1970) 756 kg, 8 wheels 10.5 km ~0.1 m/s First rover; teleoperated with signal delay
Apollo 15, 16, 17 (1971–72) LRV, crewed 27.8 / 26.6 / 35.7 km up to 3.6 m/s Highest speeds ever driven; dust fouling; dead-reckoning navigation
Luna 21 / Lunokhod 2 (1973) 840 kg 39 km ~0.14 m/s Longest robotic traverse until Opportunity; lost to overheating after dust contaminated its radiator
Chang'e-3 / Yutu (2013) 140 kg, 6 wheels 114 m 0.055 m/s Mobility system failed after ~1 lunar day
Chang'e-4 / Yutu-2 (2019– ) 140 kg > 1 km, multi-year 0.055 m/s First farside rover; extreme caution, short daily drives
Chandrayaan-3 / Pragyan (2023) 26 kg ~100 m 0.01 m/s Successful south-polar-region operation

⇒ Implications — the four hardest-won lessons:

  1. Robotic rovers are slow, and slowness is a choice. Yutu-2 travels at 0.055 m/s and covers only metres per day — not because it cannot go faster, but because ground-in-the-loop hazard assessment governs the pace. LunarSerpent's nominal speeds (0.05–0.2 m/s for robotic profiles) reflect flown reality, not vehicle top speed.
  2. Two of six rovers were disabled by mobility or thermal-dust failures, not by terrain impassability. Lunokhod 2 was lost after dust on its radiator caused overheating; Yutu's drive system failed. This validates the risk-weighted cost function of FORMULAS.md §6.4 — minimising exposure to hazardous terrain matters more than minimising distance, because failures are driven by cumulative exposure.
  3. Crewed vehicles are ~30× faster. The LRV's 3.6 m/s against Yutu-2's 0.055 m/s is why LunarSerpent's rover profiles differ by nearly two orders of magnitude, and why a traverse time prediction is meaningless without naming the vehicle.
  4. Total lunar surface distance driven by all missions ever: ~113 km. Every kilometre is expensive and irreplaceable — which is the entire justification for planning traverses as carefully as this system attempts to.

13. Claims deliberately excluded

Per the inclusion criterion of §0, these commonly repeated statements were rejected:

Claim Why excluded
"Lunar dust is toxic to humans" Inhalation risk is suspected and under study; not established for lunar regolith specifically
Precise absolute ages for individual unnamed craters Not measurable (FORMULAS.md §4.4)
"Lava tubes provide confirmed safe habitats" Pits and candidate tubes are confirmed; structural stability is not
Specific water-ice concentrations at arbitrary PSR locations Presence is confirmed; spatial distribution and concentration remain poorly constrained
"Moonquakes are a rover hazard" Deep moonquakes are real and measured, but no evidence links them to surface-vehicle risk
Global boulder density maps at metre scale No such dataset exists (§6, and PRD.md N1)

Excluding these is itself a scientific result: it marks the boundary of what this system may legitimately claim.
