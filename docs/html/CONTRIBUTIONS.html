LunarSerpent — Provenance and Contributions

Document ID: LS-DOC-009 · Version: 1.0 · Status: Baseline Date: 2026-08-09

1. Why this document exists

A scientific system is built almost entirely from other people's work. That is correct practice, not a weakness: a terramechanics model that invented its own pressure–sinkage law would be wrong, not original. But it makes one question unavoidable — what, precisely, is new here?

This document answers it explicitly, method by method, so the boundary between inherited and original is drawn deliberately rather than reconstructed afterwards. It exists to be checked.

Every method in the system falls into one of three categories:

Mark Meaning
I Inherited — used as published, cited at the point of use. No claim of novelty.
A Adapted — published method, modified for lunar or polar conditions, or for a constraint of this system. The modification is stated.
O Original — developed here. The reasoning is given so it can be disputed.

The codebase currently carries 53 distinct citations, 23 DOIs and 20 typeset formulas across 7 modules. The overwhelming majority is inherited. That proportion is expected and healthy.

Section 3 states ten original findings, each with the command that reproduces it. Where a finding was reached by first getting something wrong, that is recorded too — a result whose failure mode is documented is easier to trust than one that arrived clean.

2.2 Terrain derivatives — analysis/terrain.py

Method Source / note

2.5 Traversability — analysis/hazard.py

Method Source / note
Power-limited speed v = P / [mg(sinθ + μcosθ)] I Elementary mechanics
Slope limits for wheeled planetary vehicles I Wilcox et al. (2007), J. Field Robotics 24, 421–434
Hazard / risk / cost as three separate quantities O §3.3
Risk as 1 − (1 − r_grade)(1 − r_rough) A Standard probability of at least one of two independent events; the contribution is treating grade and roughness as independent failure modes rather than averaging them, and squaring the stability term because consequence grows faster than angle.
r_grade = max(r_stability, r_traction) — two reductions, deliberately different O Slip is a function of slope, so combining a slip term with a slope term probabilistically would count the same gradient twice — the error corrected in §3.1 and §3.3. Correlated terms are reduced by max (the binding constraint governs); only genuinely independent terms are multiplied.
Traction risk measured from the 20 % routine-slip threshold, not from zero O About 1 % slip occurs on flat ground from motion resistance alone; measuring from zero would score level terrain as hazardous.
Hazard = worse of geometric and traction verdicts, with the binding one reported O §3.2. A traction limit can be relaxed by choosing firmer ground; a stability limit cannot. An operator needs to know which they face.
Hazard classes as fractions of a specific vehicle's limits O Lets one scheme serve a 26 kg Pragyan and a 700 kg LRV without rewriting thresholds.
Cost C = (d/v)(1 + γR) with exposed risk-aversion γ O γ is a mission decision, not a physical constant, so it is a parameter rather than a tuned value.

3. Original contributions, in full

These are the claims this work would defend. Each is reproducible from the repository.

3.1 Undetrended roughness restates slope

Local elevation standard deviation is widely used as a roughness proxy. On sloping ground it is not one: a perfectly smooth plane has large elevation variance purely because it is tilted.

Measured on a constant 9.5° ramp: undetrended 1.361 m, detrended 0.000 m.

The consequence is not cosmetic. Any hazard model combining "roughness" with slope then counts the same gradient twice. In this system that error scored a gentle, entirely safe ramp at 21.6/100 against the 92.7/100 it now receives — a factor of four, on terrain a rover can drive without difficulty.

Reproduce: python -m backend.analysis.terrain for the 1.361 m / 0.000 m pair. The 21.6 figure is a development measurement: the defective code has been removed, so only the corrected score re-runs (python tests/test_planners.py).

3.2 Which constraint binds — traction or geometry — depends on the soil

Rover slope limits are conventionally quoted as geometric: a tip-over angle or a sustained-gradeability figure. Solving the Bekker–Janosi–Hanamoto system for the slip at which a vehicle can no longer generate the required drawbar pull gives a second, independent limit. Which of the two binds is not fixed — it depends on the regolith.

Rover Tip-over (geometric) Traction, nominal soil Traction, weak soil Binds on nominal Binds on weak
Pragyan-class 30.0° 30.9° 16.1° stability traction
Yutu-2-class 33.0° 39.7° 18.4° stability traction
VIPER-class 35.0° 46.5° 21.1° stability traction
Apollo LRV-class 45.0° 50.8° 23.5° stability traction

On Apollo-nominal regolith (c = 520 Pa, φ = 42°) traction gradeability exceeds every vehicle's tip-over angle, so the conventional geometric limit is the correct one and nothing is gained by modelling slip.

On the low-friction Chang'e-4 bound (c = 250 Pa, φ = 25°) that reverses for every vehicle tested. The geometric limit then over-certifies by 1.66× to 1.91× — it approves slopes at which the wheels turn without advancing. This is the failure mode that immobilised Spirit: not a grade beyond capability, but soil that could not supply the thrust.

The contribution is not "slip matters" but the sign and size of the effect, and that the answer is soil-dependent rather than a property of the vehicle. A system quoting one gradeability number for a rover is quoting an answer to an under-specified question.

On real terrain. At Connecting Ridge (89.45° S, 222° E — an Artemis III candidate region), over a 5 km square of 10 m LOLA polar data:

Nominal soil Weak soil
Passable area 99.9 % 90.9 %
Cells where traction binds 0.0 % 9.1 %
Optimal route length 6.18 km 7.60 km
Traverse time 10.3 h 13.6 h
Peak slope on route 27.0° 20.6°
Cell overlap between the two routes — 2.3 %

The soil model does not merely re-price the same route: at 2.3 % shared cells the two traverses are essentially different paths. A planner given only slope would have returned the 6.18 km route in both cases and certified 9 % of the map that the weak-soil vehicle cannot cross.

Reproduce: python -m backend.analysis.terramechanics, python tests/test_traverse_polar.py

Re-measured 2026-08-11. These figures moved slightly — traction-bound cells 9.0 → 9.1 %, the weak-soil route 7.68 → 7.60 km — after a boundary defect was fixed in the slope operator (§3.11a). The conclusion is unchanged and the shift is smaller than the spread of the soil parameters themselves, but a published number that has changed is worth saying so about.

A note on how this was found. The first implementation bisected for the traction limit over [0°, 45°] and returned 45.0° for VIPER, LRV and every other rover whose limit lay above the bound — a search ceiling presented as a measurement. It survived because 45° is a plausible-looking number. The tell was two different vehicles reporting it identically. The suite now asserts that four rovers produce four distinct limits, which fails immediately if the bound is ever reintroduced.

3.3 Hazard, risk and cost must be three quantities

Collapsing traversability into one number leaves a planner unable to distinguish illegal from merely unpleasant. This system keeps:

  • hazard — a hard constraint; impassable cells carry infinite cost and are excluded structurally, so no planner can be tuned into crossing one;
  • risk — a soft preference between legal routes, combined as independent failure modes rather than averaged;
  • cost — the objective actually minimised.

Safety scoring follows the same discipline: the peak cell governs, the mean is reported but excluded. A route that is gentle for 5 km and crosses one scarp is not safe, and averaging conceals precisely the cell that stops the vehicle. Terms multiply rather than sum, so each can veto the score alone — which is how a physical limit behaves.

A first implementation multiplied by a slope-margin term and by risk, which already contains a slope component. Removing that double-count moved the same gentle ramp from 67.6 to its present 92.7 (development measurement; the current score is verified by tests/test_planners.py).

The same ramp therefore read 21.6, then 67.6, then 92.7 as two independent double-counts were removed — from the roughness definition (§3.1) and from the score itself. Neither bug was visible as an error; each simply produced a pessimistic number that looked reasonable. That is the argument for stating what each term means before combining terms.

3.4 A*'s efficiency advantage does not survive strongly-weighted terrain

An admissible heuristic must assume the cheapest cost found anywhere on the map. Where cell costs span a wide range — as they do on any real hazard surface, because risk multiplies cost — that bound becomes loose and guides the search poorly.

Terrain Cost spread Dijkstra A* Saving
Uniform cost 1× 880 26 97 %
Random weighted 12× 1 508 1 496 1 %
Crater field (Tycho highlands) 2.1× 987 381 61 %
Open mare (Serenitatis) 2.9× 1 272 769 40 %
Highland, sustained slope 2.7× 1 375 772 44 %
Connecting Ridge, 10 m LOLA 7.8× 58 132 43 150 26 %
Crater-rim crossing 5.8× 959 838 13 %

Five real sites now, not one. The saving ranges from 61 % to 13 % and never approaches the 97 % of a uniform grid.

The saving is not a simple function of spread — the random grid has the wider spread and the smaller saving, and among the real sites the rim crossing (5.8×) saves less than the polar ridge (7.8×). Two things matter, and only one is usually discussed:

  • spread, which sets how loose the admissible bound is; and
  • spatial coherence, which sets whether cheap cells form usable corridors. Real terrain has coherent low-cost ground following ridges and crater floors, so the heuristic still points somewhere useful. Uncorrelated noise has cheap cells scattered everywhere and nowhere, and the heuristic degenerates.

The familiar claim that A* greatly outperforms Dijkstra derives from uniform or lightly-weighted grids. On real planetary hazard terrain the advantage is real but roughly a third of the textbook figure, and on synthetic random weights it nearly vanishes. Benchmarks on random grids are therefore not a guide to planetary performance in either direction.

Reproduce: python tests/test_planners.py, python tests/test_traverse_polar.py

3.5 Any-angle planning pays on real terrain and costs on synthetic terrain

Theta* is not confined to 45° headings, so it searches a strictly larger set of routes than a grid planner and its cost is not bounded below by the grid optimum. Whether it actually wins turns out to depend on how the terrain is structured, and the two cases point opposite ways:

Terrain Theta* vs grid optimum Cost spread
Random weighted grid +8.6 % (worse) 12×
Crater field (Tycho highlands) −3.7 % 2.1×
Open mare (Serenitatis) −2.0 % 2.9×
Crater-rim crossing −1.8 % 5.8×
Connecting Ridge, 10 m LOLA −2.3 % 7.8×
Highland, sustained slope 0.0 % (tie) 2.7×

Across five real sites Theta* is at or below the grid optimum every time, between 0 and 3.7 % cheaper. Only the synthetic random grid puts it above. That is one measurement repeated, not a coincidence.

A straight chord across coherent terrain — a ridge crest, a crater floor — tends to stay on ground of the same character, so the shortcut is real. A chord across uncorrelated noise samples the cost distribution afresh and reverts to the mean, losing the grid path's ability to thread between expensive cells.

The practical consequence is the 471 → 52 waypoint reduction at no cost penalty. A route a rover must actually execute as a sequence of commanded headings is nine times cheaper to represent, and each waypoint is a place the vehicle stops turning and starts driving.

Reproduce: python tests/test_traverse_polar.py

Accounting note. This comparison is only meaningful if both routes are priced on the same terrain. Summing edge costs between consecutive waypoints — correct for a grid path, where they are adjacent — silently under-prices an any-angle path, because a chord spanning 200 cells averaged from its two endpoints ignores 198 of them. Segment costs are therefore integrated cell by cell along the chord. Without that correction Theta* appeared to beat the grid optimum partly by not being charged for the ground it crossed.

3.6 Bounded-suboptimal search buys three orders of magnitude for a few per cent

Weighted A* multiplies its heuristic by ε, trading a guaranteed optimality bound of ε for speed. The bound is usually quoted; the curve rarely is. On the Connecting Ridge cost surface:

ε Cost vs optimal Expansions Fewer expansions
1.00 1.0000× 43 150 1×
1.25 1.0067× 31 685 1.4×
1.50 1.0190× 9 374 4.6×
2.00 1.0391× 464 93×
2.50 1.0589× 186 232×
4.00 1.0593× 186 232×
8.00 1.0593× 186 232×

Three things fall out that a single ε could not show.

The trade is wildly asymmetric. At ε = 2.0 the route costs 3.9 % more for 93× fewer expansions. For a planner a human is waiting on, that is not a close call.

It saturates. Past ε ≈ 2.5 the search stops changing: identical cost and identical 186 expansions at ε = 2.5, 4 and 8. The heuristic is already dominant enough to drive straight at the goal, and raising ε further buys nothing. Any implementation offering ε as a free dial is offering a range that mostly does not exist.

Report expansions, not wall-clock. Two runs of this sweep on the same machine gave identical cost ratios and identical expansion counts, while the wall-clock speed-up at ε = 2.5 came out 456× once and 232× the other — a factor of two, from nothing but machine load. The earlier version of this table quoted the timing figures. Expansions are a property of the search; seconds are a property of the afternoon.

The realised suboptimality is also far inside the guarantee — 5.9 % actual against a permitted 150 % at ε = 2.5. The bound is worst-case and, on this terrain, enormously pessimistic.

3.7 A stochastic planner's spread is the argument against it

RRT* was rejected in ALGORITHMS.md §4.2 on determinism grounds — a mission tool must give the same answer twice. That was an argument, not a measurement. Thirty seeded runs on each of five sites make it one:

Site Mean cost Best Worst σ Found
Open mare 1.081× 0.965× 1.320× 0.108 30/30
Crater field 1.008× 0.962× 1.177× 0.049 30/30
Highland 1.036× 0.998× 1.213× 0.052 30/30
Rim crossing 1.023× 0.968× 1.232× 0.065 30/30
Polar site, 10 m 1.049× 1.001× 1.123× 0.034 30/30

Completeness is not the problem — it found a route 150 times out of 150. The problem is that which route depends on the seed. On the open mare the same request returns anything from 3.5 % better than the grid optimum to 32 % worse, and nothing in the output distinguishes the good run from the bad one.

The variance is also largest where the terrain is easiest (open mare, σ = 0.108) and smallest on the hardest site (polar, σ = 0.034). Gentle ground gives the sampler many nearly-equivalent routes and no gradient to prefer between them; constrained ground funnels every sample toward the same corridor. So the one regime where a planner could most afford to be sloppy is the one where a sampling method is least repeatable.

Quoting a single run — as this project did before these thirty — would have reported any number between 0.965 and 1.320 as the result.

3.8 Resolution–hazard mismatch, quantified

Rover-scale hazards are sub-pixel in every globally available product.

Hazard Size At 474 m/px At 10 m/px
Chassis-stopping boulder 0.25 m 0.00053 px 0.025 px
Wheel step limit 0.25 m 0.00053 px 0.025 px
Rover footprint 2 m 0.0042 px 0.2 px
Traverse decision 10 m 0.021 px 1.0 px

The system therefore refuses analysis below its resolution floor rather than returning a plausible number — a 0.4 km crater is declined with "spans only 0.8 cells at 474 m/px".

3.9 Simple-cylindrical grids are degenerate at the poles

East–west cell width scales with cos(φ) and collapses as meridians converge. Slope is rise ÷ run, so a vanishing run inflates gradient without bound.

At 89.65° S the LDEM_64 east–west cell is 2.8 m against 474 m at the equator, and the slope operator returns 89.6° on ground the 10 m polar stereographic product measures at 73.3°.

Polar slope computed on an equirectangular grid is an artefact of the projection. This is why the LOLA team publishes separate polar products, and why this system uses a distinct reader for them rather than a special case.

Reproduce: python tests/test_hazard_real.py

3.10 Shadow-based boulder metrology fails at the poles in principle

Boulder height from shadow length, h = L·tan(90° − i), is standard. At polar incidence angles it becomes unusable — not through poor data, but because the sun never rises far enough.

The two NAC scenes held here are both near-grazing — M101980409LC at i = 89.79° (sun 0.21° up) and M1410768185LC at i = 89.67° (sun 0.33° up), at 0.51 m/px:

  • a 1 m boulder casts a 273 m shadow in the first scene and 174 m in the second, overlapping every other shadow in the frame, so a dark patch cannot be attributed to a single rock;
  • ±2 px of shadow-length error → 0.4 cm of height error — negligible, which is the surprise: the measurement one would expect to dominate does not;
  • ±0.1° of incidence error → 0.48 m of height error, 48 % of a 1 m boulder.

The entire error budget sits in the illumination geometry, not in the image. Sharper pixels cannot fix this; only a higher sun can.

Boulder width, measured from the sunlit face, carries no such sensitivity. Height by shadow requires 50–80° incidence, which does not occur at the poles.

3.11 Defects found by testing claims rather than code

Every finding above was reached by measurement, which means a defect in the measuring apparatus is a defect in the finding. Two were found this way and are recorded because the numbers in §3 moved when they were fixed.

3.11a — slope was halved around every window edge.

A 3×3 finite-difference operator at the boundary of an array has no neighbour outside it. With mode="nearest" the edge row is duplicated, the difference across it is taken over half the true distance, and the reported gradient is half the real one.

Measured on a constant 5.7106° ramp: interior 5.7106°, first and last column 2.8624°. On real polar terrain the border read 0.90× the interior mean, across 1.62 % of cells.

The fix is to read one cell of margin, differentiate, then trim — so every cell in the region of interest is computed with neighbours on all four sides. The margin now lives inside analyse_traversability rather than in its callers, because two of the three callers had forgotten it, and those two were the ones producing published numbers.

Reproduce: python tests/test_plan_service.py — asserts a haloed ramp is uniform to its edge, and that the untrimmed version would be wrong.

3.11b — the projection-distortion guard inverted past the antipode.

Azimuthal equidistant stretches tangentially as c / sin(c). Past the antipode (c > π) that expression turns negative, so a guard using it to reject over-large regions reported a 10,818 km region as less distorted than a 700 km one and admitted it — while correctly refusing a smaller 7,221 km one.

The failure is silent and inverted: the check passes exactly the cases it exists to stop. It now returns infinity beyond the antipode, and regions are capped at 2,603 km across, where distortion reaches 10 %.

Reproduce: python -m backend.core.projection.

4. Datasets

All source data is public-domain NASA, USGS, PDS or ISRO material. No dataset in this repository is original; the catalogues, DEMs and imagery are cited in DATASETS.md with producer, instrument, resolution and reference frame.

The derived indexes (Robbins → memory-mapped array; IAU gazetteer → JSON; Robbins ↔ IAU spatial join) are transformations of published data, reproducible from the scripts in scripts/.

5. How this work should be cited

If any of §3 is used, cite the finding rather than the software, and state the version. The claims are reproducible from a tagged commit; the code is a means of reproducing them, not the contribution itself.

Inherited methods must retain their original citations — Bekker, Janosi & Hanamoto, Wong & Reece, Horn, Pike, Snyder, Dijkstra, Hart et al., Carrier et al. A reader should be able to reach the primary source for every equation without passing through this project.
